\begin{abstract}
This report presents a implementation and empirical analysis of Input-Convex Neural Networks (ICNNs) applied to continuous Optimal Transport (OT) under quadratic cost ($W_2$), reproducing the minimax dual formulation by Makkuva et al. (2020). The codebase is modularized into reusable components (\texttt{src/}) validated by a suite of 46 automated unit tests (\texttt{tests/}). I validate the solver against analytical 2D Gaussian mappings (achieving a 0.88\% relative $W_2$ error) and evaluate mapping quality on the official NeurIPS 2021 Korotin continuous Wasserstein-2 benchmark (\texttt{Mix3ToMix10}) across dimensions $D \in \{2, 4, 8, 16, 32\}$. Following research directives, I isolate three potential failure causes: (1) function-class expressivity, (2) optimization instability, and (3) computational cost. Controlled ablations at $D=16$ show that scaling model capacity by $48\times$ (from 11k to 550k parameters) yields zero improvement in transport accuracy (L2-UVP remaining flat $\sim 36.5\% - 41.0\%$), indicating that capacity is not the bottleneck. Trajectory telemetry demonstrates that \textbf{rapidly increasing transient gradient norms (peaking at 12,999 in 32D) and growth of the $g$-player objective} are strongly associated with high-dimensional mapping accuracy degradation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Optimal Transport (OT) provides a mathematical framework for comparing, aligning, and pushing forward probability measures. Computing the Monge map---the transport map $T: \mathbb{R}^D \to \mathbb{R}^D$ minimizing quadratic transport cost---requires enforcing cyclical monotonicity. Under Brenier's theorem, this map is uniquely characterized as the gradient of a convex potential function: $T(x) = \nabla \varphi(x)$. Input-Convex Neural Networks (ICNNs), introduced by Amos et al. (2017), solve this by constraining hidden layer weights to be non-negative and using convex, non-decreasing activation functions.

This project delivers:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A fully modularized PyTorch codebase (\texttt{src/}) protected by 46 automated unit tests (\texttt{tests/}).
    \item Directional consistency between source distributions ($\mu$), target distributions ($\nu$), forward maps ($T = \nabla f$), and conjugate inverse maps ($S = \nabla g$).
    \item High-dimensional evaluation on the official NeurIPS 2021 Korotin \texttt{Mix3ToMix10} benchmark ($D \in \{2, 4, 8, 16, 32\}$).
    \item Audited quantitative optimization telemetry capturing loss and gradient trajectories for both player potentials ($f$ and $g$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Theoretical Framework \& Formulations}

\subsection{Kantorovich Duality \& Brenier's Theorem}
Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be probability measures on $\mathbb{R}^D$ with finite second moments. The Wasserstein-2 distance under quadratic cost is:
\begin{equation}
W_2^2(\mu, \nu) = \inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu, \nu)} \mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim \pi} \left[ \|x - y\|^2 \right]
\end{equation}
By Brenier's Theorem, if $\mu$ is continuous, there exists a unique optimal transport map $T(x) = \nabla \varphi(x)$ pushing forward $\mu$ to $\nu$ ($T_\# \mu = \nu$).

Using Fenchel-Rockafellar duality, the quadratic OT cost translates to the dual formulation:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{2} W_2^2(\mu, \nu) = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu}[\|x\|^2] + \frac{1}{2} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \nu}[\|y\|^2] - \inf_{\varphi \in \text{CVX}} \left( \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu}[\varphi(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \nu}[\varphi^*(y)] \right)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Minimax Dual Formulation (Makkuva et al., 2020)}
Makkuva et al. parameterize both potential $f \approx \varphi$ and conjugate $g \approx \varphi^*$ using two separate ICNNs, formulating a minimax game:
\begin{equation}
\min_{f \in \text{CVX}} \max_{g \in \text{CVX}} L(f, g) := \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu}[f(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \nu} \left[ \langle y, \nabla g(y) \rangle - f(\nabla g(y)) \right]
\end{equation}

\subsection{Directional Consistency}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Forward Map}: $T(x) = \nabla f(x)$ maps samples from source distribution $\mu$ to target distribution $\nu$ ($T_\# \mu = \nu$).
    \item \textbf{Inverse Map}: $S(y) = \nabla g(y)$ maps samples from target distribution $\nu$ back to source distribution $\mu$ ($S_\# \nu = \mu$).
\end{itemize}

\section{Telemetry Instrumentation Audit Verification}
Prior to drawing conclusions from gradient trajectory metrics, the solver's instrumentation in \texttt{src/solver.py} was verified:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Gradient Zeroing}: \texttt{opt\_g.zero\_grad()} and \texttt{opt\_f.zero\_grad()} are executed before every backward pass.
    \item \textbf{Measurement Timing}: Gradient norms are computed immediately after \texttt{loss.backward()} and before \texttt{optimizer.step()}.
    \item \textbf{Exact L2 Norm Formula}: $\|\nabla_\theta L\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i} \|\nabla_{\theta_i} L\|_2^2}$.
    \item \textbf{Player Separation}: $f$ and $g$ parameter gradient norms are measured separately.
    \item \textbf{No Gradient Clipping}: Gradients are unclipped and unnormalized (\texttt{clip\_weights()} clips weight parameters $W_z \ge 0$ post-step).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
Under the tested architecture, training budget, and minimax optimization settings, transport accuracy degrades substantially with dimension. This degradation is strongly associated with increasingly unstable minimax optimization dynamics, as evidenced by rapidly growing transient gradient norms and growth of the $g$-player objective.

\begin{abstract}
This paper presents a PyTorch implementation, unit-tested software architecture, and empirical investigation of Input-Convex Neural Networks (ICNNs) applied to continuous Optimal Transport (OT) under quadratic cost ($W_2$), reproducing the minimax dual formulation by Makkuva et al. (2020). The software codebase is modularized into decoupled components under \texttt{src/} and protected by 53 automated unit tests. We validate the solver against analytical 2D Gaussian transport maps and evaluate mapping quality on the official NeurIPS 2021 Korotin continuous Wasserstein-2 benchmark (\texttt{Mix3ToMix10}) across dimensions $D \in \{2, 4, 8, 16, 32\}$. A $D=16$ width ablation scales capacity by $48\times$ without systematic transport improvement. An oracle paired-map control removes the two-player game and reduces peak parameter-gradient norms from 2,685.76/12,999.47 to 1.08/1.39 at $D=16/32$, while unequal player learning rates also strongly suppress minimax gradient spikes. However, neither intervention fully recovers high-dimensional transport accuracy. The resulting evidence supports minimax dynamics as a major source of severe optimization instability, while leaving the clipped ICNN parameterization, conditioning, approximation, and training budget as plausible additional contributors to the remaining error.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Optimal Transport (OT) provides a mathematical framework for comparing, aligning, and pushing forward probability measures. While discrete OT formulations suffer from cubic computational complexity with respect to sample size, continuous OT parameterizes transport maps using deep neural networks, enabling applications in generative modeling (VAEs, GANs), domain adaptation, and computational biology.

Computing the Monge map---the transport map $T: \mathbb{R}^D \to \mathbb{R}^D$ minimizing quadratic transport cost---requires enforcing cyclical monotonicity. Under Brenier's theorem, this map is uniquely characterized as the gradient of a convex potential function: $T(x) = \nabla \varphi(x)$. Standard deep neural networks cannot guarantee convexity in their inputs. Input-Convex Neural Networks (ICNNs), introduced by Amos et al. (2017), solve this by constraining hidden layer weights to be non-negative and using convex, non-decreasing activation functions.

This project delivers:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A fully modularized PyTorch codebase (\texttt{src/}) protected by 53 automated unit tests (\texttt{tests/}).
    \item Directional consistency between source distributions ($\mu$), target distributions ($\nu$), forward maps ($T = \nabla f$), and conjugate inverse maps ($S = \nabla g$).
    \item High-dimensional evaluation on the official NeurIPS 2021 Korotin \texttt{Mix3ToMix10} benchmark ($D \in \{2, 4, 8, 16, 32\}$).
    \item Audited quantitative optimization telemetry capturing loss and gradient trajectories for both player potentials ($f$ and $g$).
    \item Controlled failure-mode isolation experiments evaluating width, an oracle paired-map objective, and unequal player learning rates.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Theoretical Framework \& Mathematical Formulations}

\subsection{Kantorovich Duality \& Brenier's Theorem}
Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be probability measures on $\mathbb{R}^D$ with finite second moments. The Wasserstein-2 distance under quadratic cost is:
\begin{equation}
W_2^2(\mu, \nu) = \inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu, \nu)} \mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim \pi} \left[ \|x - y\|^2 \right]
\end{equation}
where $\Pi(\mu, \nu)$ is the set of joint distributions (couplings) whose marginals are $\mu$ and $\nu$. By Brenier's Theorem, if $\mu$ is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, there exists a unique optimal transport map $T(x) = \nabla \varphi(x)$ pushing forward $\mu$ to $\nu$ ($T_\# \mu = \nu$), where $\varphi: \mathbb{R}^D \to \mathbb{R}$ is a convex function.

Using Fenchel-Rockafellar duality, the quadratic OT cost translates to the dual formulation:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{2} W_2^2(\mu, \nu) = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu}[\|x\|^2] + \frac{1}{2} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \nu}[\|y\|^2] - \inf_{\varphi \in \text{CVX}} \left( \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu}[\varphi(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \nu}[\varphi^*(y)] \right)
\end{equation}
where $\varphi^*(y) = \sup_{z \in \mathbb{R}^D} \langle y, z \rangle - \varphi(z)$ denotes the Fenchel conjugate of $\varphi$.

\subsection{Minimax Dual Formulation (Makkuva et al., 2020)}
Evaluating the exact Fenchel conjugate $\varphi^*$ analytically is intractable for general high-dimensional distributions. Makkuva et al. bypass explicit conjugation by parameterizing both the potential $f \approx \varphi$ and its conjugate $g \approx \varphi^*$ using two separate ICNNs.

Since $\nabla \varphi^* = (\nabla \varphi)^{-1}$, the inverse transport map pushing $\nu \to \mu$ is given by $S(y) = \nabla g(y)$. The dual OT problem is formulated as a zero-sum minimax game between potential $f$ and conjugate potential $g$:
\begin{equation}
\min_{f \in \text{CVX}} \max_{g \in \text{CVX}} L(f, g) := \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu}[f(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \nu} \left[ \langle y, \nabla g(y) \rangle - f(\nabla g(y)) \right]
\end{equation}
The inner maximization over $g$ enforces that $g$ approximates the Fenchel conjugate $f^*$, while the outer minimization over $f$ minimizes the total transport cost.

\subsection{Directional Consistency of Mappings}
To ensure rigorous mathematical alignment across all experiments:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Forward Map}: $T(x) = \nabla f(x)$ maps samples from source distribution $\mu$ to target distribution $\nu$ ($T_\# \mu = \nu$).
    \item \textbf{Inverse Map}: $S(y) = \nabla g(y)$ maps samples from target distribution $\nu$ back to source distribution $\mu$ ($S_\# \nu = \mu$).
\end{itemize}
In the multimodal mixture experiment, $f$ pushes unimodal Gaussian source samples $x \sim \mu = \mathcal{N}(0, 0.8^2 I_2)$ forward into the 8 distinct mixture modes of $\nu$, whereas $\nabla g(y)$ maps mixture target samples $y \sim \nu$ back into the unimodal source.

\section{Modular Architecture \& Telemetry Audit}

\subsection{Software Scaffolding (\texttt{src/})}
The codebase is structured into reusable, decoupled Python modules under \texttt{src/}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{src/icnn.py}: Contains \texttt{ICNN} and \texttt{StandardMLP} network architectures. Implements \texttt{clip\_weights()} clamping $W_z \ge 0$ post-step and \texttt{grad(y)} wrapped in \texttt{torch.enable\_grad()} to enable gradient evaluations inside \texttt{no\_grad} contexts.
    \item \texttt{src/losses.py}: Implements the dual \texttt{minimax\_loss(f, g, x\_mu, y\_nu)}.
    \item \texttt{src/solver.py}: Implements \texttt{train\_icnn\_ot} with alternating inner/outer optimization steps and iteration-level telemetry logging.
    \item \texttt{src/samplers.py}: Implements distribution samplers for Gaussian, Gaussian Mixture, Disconnected, and Finite Pool sources.
    \item \texttt{src/metrics.py}: Implements \texttt{compute\_official\_l2\_uvp} measuring L2 Unexplained Variance Percentage and Cosine Similarity.
    \item \texttt{src/benchmark.py}: Dynamic benchmark loader supporting environment-configurable paths (\texttt{WASSERSTEIN\_BENCHMARK\_PATH}).
\end{itemize}
The module is protected by \textbf{53 automated unit tests} in \texttt{tests/}.

\subsection{Telemetry Instrumentation Audit Verification}
Prior to analyzing gradient trajectory metrics, the solver's instrumentation in \texttt{src/solver.py} was audited:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Gradient Zeroing}: \texttt{opt\_g.zero\_grad()} and \texttt{opt\_f.zero\_grad()} are explicitly executed before every backward pass.
    \item \textbf{Measurement Timing}: Gradient norms are computed immediately after \texttt{loss.backward()} and before \texttt{optimizer.step()}.
    \item \textbf{Exact L2 Norm Formula}: $\|\nabla_\theta L\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i} \|\nabla_{\theta_i} L\|_2^2}$.
    \item \textbf{Player Separation}: $f$ and $g$ parameter gradient norms are measured separately.
    \item \textbf{No Gradient Clipping}: Gradients are unclipped and unnormalized (\texttt{clip\_weights()} clips weight parameters $W_z \ge 0$ post-step).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Detailed Optimization Dynamics Discussion}

The quantitative trajectory telemetry allows us to evaluate the high-dimensional degradation mechanisms:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Transient Gradient Norm Spikes}: Peak parameter-gradient norms for player $f$ increase rapidly with dimension---from $10.80$ at $D=2$ to $2,685.76$ at $D=16$ and $12,999.47$ at $D=32$. The $g$-player gradient norm is separately measured on the final inner $g$ update of each outer iteration.
    \item \textbf{Growth of the $g$-Player Objective}: The raw $g$-player objective grows from $8.82$ in 2D to $300.40$ in 32D, consistent with an increasingly difficult inner maximization. This quantity is not interpreted as a duality gap.
    \item \textbf{Outer Loss Trajectory Divergence}: At $D=16$ and $D=32$, $f$-loss increases during optimization ($6.62 \to 10.71$ in 16D; $9.11 \to 16.55$ in 32D, an 82\% increase). The solver moves away from initial potential configurations.
    \item \textbf{Stability versus Accuracy}: Oracle regression removes the large spikes and improves transport metrics, while slow-$g$ minimax updates suppress spikes by up to 97.5\% at D=32. Yet the most stable D=32 slow-$g$ run has the worst L2-UVP. Gradient stabilization is therefore not by itself sufficient to recover high-dimensional accuracy.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
Under the tested architecture, training budget, and minimax optimization settings, transport accuracy degrades substantially with dimension. The combined width, oracle, and unequal-learning-rate diagnostics provide strong empirical evidence that minimax dynamics are a major contributor to severe high-dimensional gradient instability. They do not prove that instability is the sole cause of degraded transport accuracy: the oracle experiment remains materially inaccurate despite stable gradients, and the most stable unequal-LR configuration at D=32 is not the most accurate.

The next research decision should be made with the advisor: either evaluate one carefully specified minimax stabilizer (such as a gradient penalty or EMA) with repeated seeds, or investigate an alternative convex-potential parameterization. The present evidence does not support claiming that the minimax game alone explains the high-dimensional error.
